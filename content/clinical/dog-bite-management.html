Dog Bite & Animal Bite Injuries: Facial Wound Care and Repair

Bite wounds to the face need careful management. They carry a higher risk of infection than many other injuries, and because the face is visible and functionally important, how the wound is closed affects both appearance and function afterward.

Immediate First Aid

Wash the wound thoroughly with soap and running water as soon as possible, and control any active bleeding with gentle direct pressure. Seek medical attention promptly rather than waiting, since both the wound itself and the risk of infection, including the need for rabies post exposure prophylaxis assessed by the treating physician according to current protocol, are time sensitive.

Surgical Management

Assessment includes checking the depth of the wound and whether deeper structures such as nerves, the salivary duct, or muscle are involved. Depending on how contaminated the wound is and how much time has passed since the injury, closure may be immediate or delayed to allow the wound to be adequately cleaned first. Careful, layered closure technique is used on facial wounds specifically to support the best possible cosmetic and functional result.

Infection Prevention

Bite wounds carry bacteria from the animal's mouth, so antibiotics are commonly part of treatment. Tetanus vaccination status is checked and updated if needed, and a follow up review is scheduled to monitor healing and catch any early signs of infection.

Facial bite wounds are best assessed promptly. Contact the clinic as soon as possible after the injury.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should a dog bite wound be stitched immediately?

Not always. The decision depends on how contaminated the wound is and how much time has passed since the injury. Some wounds are closed immediately, while others are cleaned and monitored first, with delayed closure, to reduce infection risk.

Do all dog bites need rabies treatment?

This depends on the circumstances of the bite and current medical protocol, and is assessed by the treating physician, considering the animal involved and local guidelines. It should not be assumed either way without assessment.

Will a facial dog bite leave a scar?

Some degree of scarring is possible with any facial wound, but careful, layered closure technique is used specifically to minimise this and support the best functional and cosmetic outcome.

How soon should I seek treatment after a bite?

As soon as possible. Prompt wound cleaning and assessment reduce infection risk and support a better outcome, and time sensitive decisions such as rabies risk assessment are part of early care.

Can a bite injure a nerve or the salivary duct?

Yes, depending on the location and depth of the bite. This is one reason a careful clinical assessment of the wound is important before closure, so that any injury to deeper structures is identified and addressed.
